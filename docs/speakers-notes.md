## Chapter 1 — Prologue (Slide 1)
**On screen:** "Hackathon. Two days. Three teams. Fifteen brains."
**Time budget:** ~1.5 min

**Open with the room. Set the scene.**

> Good morning. Thank you for having me.
>
> Last November, fifteen people from the Swedish Tax Agency walked through the glass doors of Google's Stockholm office. The room smelled like coffee and maybe a little bit of nerdiness.
>
> We had two days. No preparation. One question: can we get multiple AI agents to actually collaborate — not just answer questions, but work together like a team?
>
> We weren't building for production. We were building to understand. To feel where the edges are.
>
> Three teams. Three wildly different approaches. And what came out of those 48 hours changed how we think about AI at Skatteverket.

> We weren't building for perfection. We were building to understand the behaviour, the limits, and the possibilities of what we now call "agentic AI."

## Chapter 3 — The Swarm (Slide 3)
**On screen:** Team Alpha stats, agent cards, blueprint button
**Time budget:** ~4 min (the centerpiece)

**This is the dramatic peak. Build slowly. Use the sub-steps.**

> In Sweden, more than ten thousand influencers make a living from brand deals and gifts. Most want to do the right thing — but tax rules aren't always easy to interpret. The result? People slip into the hidden economy without even knowing it.
>
> So Team Alpha asked: can we detect these risks automatically — before it becomes a problem for the influencer?
>
> They built five agents. One scrapes social media feeds. A valuation agent estimates the worth of luxury items — watches, handbags, even cars spotted in YouTube videos. Risk profilers consolidate everything into a compliance report.
>
> What could take a human analyst days was done in minutes.
>
> But here's the part that made the jury lean forward: when the team showed how the same approach could be adapted for crime prevention — scanning for stolen goods, cross-referencing declared income with visible assets — that changed the conversation entirely.

## Chapter 4 — The Pedagogy (Slide 4)
**On screen:** "The Knowledge Split" — What vs How
**Time budget:** ~2.5 min

**This is the lesson about trust and accuracy.**

> Team Bravo tried something different. Every day, the Tax Information Service gets thousands of calls. People just want to know: what applies to me?
>
> So the team built Skatti 2.0 — an AI agent trained on our official website. It should read, interpret, and give correct answers. 24/7. Never tired.
>
> And at first it seemed to work. But then they noticed something.
>
> The answers were... shaky. Sometimes confidently wrong. The agent trained on official sources actually performed worse than a plain language model.
>
> Why? Because the official site explains *what* you need to do — but not *how*. And the web is full of sites explaining "how" — some right, some wrong. The agent couldn't tell the difference.
>
> *(Gesture at the two panels on screen.)*
>
> "The What" — high trust. Official. Statutory facts.
> "The How" — high risk of hallucination. Confident, but wrong.
>
> The team's conclusion? For precise legal answers: not good enough. But for reasoning, guidance, and pedagogy — excellent help.
>
> Skatti 2.0 turned out to be a promising colleague. Just one you occasionally have to correct. *(Pause, smile.)* "Skatti… you're actually making that up."

**Transition cue:** Let the laugh settle. Then scroll.

## Chapter 5 — The Automation (Slide 5)
**On screen:** Data sources, speed comparison
**Time budget:** ~2 min

**Keep this tight. It's the efficiency argument.**

> Team Delta went after something different: risk assessment at scale.
>
> Every year, Skatteverket evaluates thousands of companies — which ones look normal, which ones deserve closer inspection. This involves downloading annual reports, cross-referencing Statistics Sweden, checking public registries. It is important work. But it's also work where humans easily drown in data.
>
> So they built a swarm. One agent pulls annual reports. Another fetches SCB statistics. A third gathers public information. And then an analysis agent produces a preliminary risk evaluation.
>
> *(Point at the comparison bar on screen.)*
>
> Hours of human compilation — versus seconds of AI reasoning.
>
> The technical solution combined serial and parallel agent execution. Each agent knew exactly when to step in. Like a small digital team.
>
> And the result? It didn't feel like an experiment. It felt like the first draft of future risk analysis. This wasn't science fiction. It was a prototype that could already make a difference.

**Transition cue:** Scroll.

## Chapter 7 — The Wisdom (Slide 7)
**On screen:** Core insight quote, AI evolution progression
**Time budget:** ~3 min

**This is the thesis of the entire talk. Slow down. Deliver it with weight.**

> So what did we actually learn?
>
> *(Let the quote appear on screen. Read it slowly.)*
>
> Agents are not just technology. They're teammates without judgment. They need boundaries, orchestration, and patience.
>
> One agent is a tool. You ask, it answers. But multiple agents with roles and responsibilities? That becomes a workforce.
>
> And here's the thing we didn't expect: the hardest part wasn't the technology. The hardest part was the structure. Deciding who does what. When to split work. When to converge. How to verify.
>
> *(Gesture at the AI evolution on screen.)*
>
> We're on a journey. We started with AI Chat — reactive, one question at a time. Then we moved to AI Assistants — context-aware, helpful, but still passive.
>
> Where we're going? AI Coworkers. Agents that don't just respond, but act, integrate, and orchestrate workflows on their own.
>
> That's Skatteverket 3.0. And through 'Kraftsamling AI' — our internal initiative — we're building the foundation. Culture. Legal compliance. Entirely new ways of working.

**Transition cue:** Pause. Take a breath. Then scroll to the final slide.

>
> Where do we draw the line between automation and decision-making?
>
> What does "good enough" even mean when the output affects real people?
>
> *(Pause. Look at the audience.)*

> Because agents need boundaries, orchestration, and patience.
>
> *(Beat.)*
>
> And apparently... so do we.
>
> *(Hold the silence for 2-3 seconds. Then:)*
>
> And that's exactly where the journey continues. Thank you.
